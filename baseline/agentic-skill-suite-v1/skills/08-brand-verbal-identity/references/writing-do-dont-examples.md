# Reference: writing usable DO/DON'T examples instead of vague adjectives

Used in `SKILL.md` steps 3 and 7 (principle derivation and the multi-channel
example set).

## The test a DO/DON'T pair must pass
A pair is usable only if a writer who has never seen the brand before could,
from the pair alone, correctly classify a *third*, new line of copy as
on-voice or off-voice. If the pair only makes sense with the principle's
adjective already in mind, it isn't doing any work.

## Format
Always pair, never list a DO alone:
```
DO: "[actual line of copy, not a description of copy]"
DON'T: "[actual line of copy that plausibly could have been written, that violates the principle]"
```

The DON'T must be a **plausible temptation**, not a strawman. "DON'T: 'BUY
NOW!!! LIMITED TIME!!!'" teaches nothing if the brand would never write that
regardless of voice. A good DON'T is the generic, competent, on-topic
version of the line that a writer would reach for by default — the version
this specific principle exists to override.

## Worked conversion

Bad:
> Voice: friendly, approachable, expert.

Better, still failing the test (adjective restated as a claim):
> DO: "We're here to help, and we know our stuff."
> DON'T: "Contact support for assistance."

The DON'T isn't a real temptation — no brand voice guide would produce that
robotic line by default, so the pair proves nothing about what makes *this*
brand distinct.

Passing the test (specific, plausible temptation, teaches a real choice):
> **Principle: Answer the question before the pleasantry.**
> DO: "Yes — refunds land in 3–5 business days. Anything else?"
> DON'T: "Thanks so much for reaching out! We'd be happy to help — refunds
> typically take 3–5 business days depending on your bank."
The DON'T is exactly what a competent, generic support-copy default sounds
like. The pair shows the actual editorial choice: lead with the answer.

## Channel breadth requirement
A principle's DO/DON'T pair in one channel (e.g. only homepage headlines)
does not prove the principle survives contact with reality. Write pairs
across formats that pull in different directions — a short high-stakes
format (error message) and a longer low-stakes one (social caption) at
minimum — so the principle isn't secretly just "how to write headlines."

## Common failure: the pair is really about grammar, not voice
"DO: use contractions. DON'T: don't." is a style-guide rule, not a voice
principle — it belongs in `VERBAL_IDENTITY.md`'s syntax/rhythm section, not
presented as one of the 3–5 core behavioral principles. Keep voice
principles about *editorial choices* (what to say, what to lead with, what
to leave out), and syntax conventions about *mechanics* (sentence length,
contractions, numerals) — don't let the mechanics section masquerade as
the whole voice.
