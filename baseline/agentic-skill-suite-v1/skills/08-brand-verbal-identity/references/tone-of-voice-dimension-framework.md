# Reference: tone-of-voice dimensions and how to convert them into behavioral rules

Used in `SKILL.md` step 3 (Derive behavioral voice principles).

## Why dimensions, not adjectives
An adjective ("bold") has no operational content — two writers will produce
different, equally defensible copy from it. A **dimension position**, backed
by a **behavioral rule**, does: it tells a writer what to do differently
than the alternative.

## The four core dimensions
Use these as a starting checklist, not a fill-in-the-blank form — only
include a dimension in `VERBAL_IDENTITY.md` if the strategy actually gives
you a reason to pick a position on it.

1. **Formal ↔ Casual** — degree of professional distance vs. familiarity.
2. **Serious ↔ Playful** — how much levity the brand permits, and where.
3. **Matter-of-fact ↔ Enthusiastic** — how much the copy editorializes vs.
   states plainly.
4. **Respectful/deferential ↔ Irreverent** — how much the brand defers to
   category conventions and authority vs. pokes at them.

## Converting a dimension position into a behavioral rule
A position on a dimension ("mostly matter-of-fact, slightly playful") is
still not operational on its own. Convert it by answering: **what does the
brand do instead of the generic alternative, and why does the strategy
demand that?**

Bad (adjective, no rule):
> Voice is "confident."

Better (dimension position, still not fully operational):
> Voice sits toward the matter-of-fact end — states things plainly rather
> than hyping them.

Operational (behavioral rule, traced, with DO/DON'T):
> **Principle: State the number, skip the adjective.** Traces to: strategy's
> differentiator is verified lab results, not brand feeling — the audience
> (compliance-anxious buyers) trusts specificity over enthusiasm.
> DO: "Reduces packing time by 40% in third-party testing."
> DON'T: "Our revolutionary process saves you tons of time!"

## Deriving a position from strategy, not preference
For each dimension you decide to use, answer:
- What does the target audience already distrust or reward in this category?
  (e.g. a compliance-anxious B2B buyer rewards matter-of-fact; a streetwear
  audience rewards irreverence toward category convention.)
- What does the differentiator require the brand to sound like to be
  credible? A brand differentiated on rigor cannot sound breezy about the
  thing it's rigorous about, even if it's playful elsewhere.
- Where does the category default sit, and is breaking from it a deliberate
  strategic choice or just noise? Only break from category default when the
  strategy gives a reason to.

If you cannot answer at least the first question for a given dimension from
`BRAND_STRATEGY.md`, do not assert a position on it — leave it out rather
than guessing.
